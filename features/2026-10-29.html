Dominant Themes

  • AI Infrastructure’s Unsustainable Capex and Margin Pressures: Multiple sources highlight the massive compute commitments by AI firms (Anthropic’s $800B, Meta’s $140B CapEx) driving near-term margin compression and cash flow strain, despite strong demand. This raises concerns about long-term profitability and funding sustainability (see [6], [22], [27], [58], [72]).
  • Inflation Regime Split: Services vs. Goods: A consensus emerges around bifurcated U.S. inflation—goods disinflation vs. sticky services inflation above 4%—driven by labor costs, leading to a “higher-for-longer” rate outlook and bearishness on long-duration bonds (see [21], [64], [70]).
  • Structural Tightness in Energy Markets: Non-OPEC supply stagnation, Middle East outages, and geopolitical supply cuts (e.g., Indonesia to Singapore) point to persistent oil and regional energy deficits, supporting higher prices and favoring exposure to tight supply dynamics (see [36], [55], [67]).
  • AI Disruption Threat to Software Monopolies: Several analysts argue that AI agents and clones will erode the pricing power and subscription models of dominant software firms like Microsoft and Adobe, compressing margins and threatening long-term revenue models (see [47], [49], [72]).
  • Deep Value Pools in Regional Equities: Canadian and European markets show concentrated pockets of undervaluation—Canadian stocks with high FCF yields and low multiples, and European compounders in niche industrials and materials—suggesting selective opportunity amid broader skepticism (see [30], [9], [33], [7]).

Cross-Currents & Disagreements

  • AI’s Inflationary vs. Deflationary Impact: One camp ([20], [72]) argues AI capex and infrastructure demand are inflationary, pushing yields higher. The opposing view ([Crack the Market, Oct 23]) sees AI-driven efficiency and falling per-unit compute costs as deflationary, pressuring margins and valuations—directly conflicting on macro direction.
  • Anthropic’s Profitability Narrative: Some sources ([6], [58]) emphasize strong unit economics and 85% gross margins as proof of AI monetization viability. Others ([24], [25], [72]) highlight massive noncash losses, opaque disclosures, and circular accounting, suggesting the profitability narrative is overstated or misleading.

Non-Consensus / Contrarian Flag

The most contrarian call is [47]’s argument that AI will destroy the quasi-monopoly pricing power of Microsoft and Adobe, not through incremental competition but via low-cost, functionally equivalent clones. While most AI analysis focuses on winners (enablers, infrastructure), this view flips the script by identifying major *losers* in the software ecosystem—firms whose high-margin, entrenched models are uniquely vulnerable to AI-driven disintermediation.
